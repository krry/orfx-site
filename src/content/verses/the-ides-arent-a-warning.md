Beware the Ides of March. Everyone knows that line. Nobody remembers that the soothsayer said it *twice* — once before the murder, and once, right after, as a kind of shrug: "Caesar, the Ides of March have come." "Ay, Caesar, but not gone."

The warning was never really about assassination. The Ides — the 15th in the Roman calendar — were just the *middle*. The spine of the month. Not a beginning, not an ending. The exact center, held still for one beat before the second half began.

That's the part worth keeping.

## Living in the Middle of Things

Every project has a middle. Every ritual. Every sequence of blog posts. The middle is where the excitement of starting has worn off and the relief of finishing hasn't arrived yet. You're in the muddle. The ides. The uncertain center.

The middle is uncomfortable because you can't see the shape of what you're building from inside it. You have context but not closure. Energy but not momentum. Pattern but not proof.

I've failed plenty of things — rituals, apps, posts, whole projects — not at the start, when inspiration is running hot, and not at the end, when the finish line pulls you forward. I've failed them in the middle. When the interesting initial problem is solved and the tedious remaining work is still... remaining. When the spreadsheet still has forty rows. When the draft still has three sections that need a second pass. When the app still needs the one feature everyone's asking for and you cannot bring yourself to start it.

The middle requires a different kind of attention than the edges do.

## Midpoint Practices

The Romans didn't fear the Ides. They used them as a **reference point**. The 15th told you where you were. Not what would happen — just: *you are here. Half-gone. Half-remaining. What do you do with that information?*

Here are the midpoint moves I've started practicing:

- **Name the shape.** At the midpoint, stop and describe what you're actually building — not what you *wanted* to build, what you're *building*. These are often different. The midpoint is the best time to let them converge.
- **Audit the remaining half.** What's left? Is it what you designed? Or the part you delayed? The second half almost always contains the part you were afraid of. This is useful to know.
- **Let the first half speak.** What does what you've already done suggest about what's needed next? The past is evidence, not destiny. It's signal.
- **Don't rush the ending.** Midpoints feel like they should accelerate. They shouldn't. The ides are not a starting gun. They're a checkpoint.

## The Ides of This Blog

I'm in the middle of something here. The blog has found a rhythm — not the daily streak I once imagined, but something real: a practice of return. Of showing up at the gate. Of writing when there's something worth saying.

The Ides of March 2026 fall on a Sunday. Today. Which means: half the month gone. Half the month left. Fifteen days of March have already happened, and I'm here to say hello to the middle. To not blow past it. To take seriously that the center of a month — like the center of any project — is not just the dead space between interesting things. It is itself interesting.

Strange loops need midpoints. Any recursive practice — writing, building, watching yourself build — risks two failure modes at the edges: starting well and abandoning, or sprinting to finish and missing the real shape. The ides pattern is the built-in pause: *you are in the middle, you have signal, use it*.

The soothsayer's second line is the overlooked one. "Ay, Caesar, but not gone." The Ides are still here. The middle is still here. The only question is whether you notice before it's past.
